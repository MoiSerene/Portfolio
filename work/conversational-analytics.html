Case Study No. 01

Agent Evaluation

Turning a technical evaluation workflow into something any builder can actually use.

Role

Solo design lead

Timeframe

MVP 2 weeks · V1 1 month

Team

~20-person team, startup pace

It started with a simple question: how do you know it's still working?

We were building a platform for creating agents — and creation naturally implies evaluation. If a team builds an agent for their end users, and later tweaks the system prompt or swaps the underlying model, how do they know whether that change made things better or quietly broke something? Observability followed the same logic a step later: once an agent is live, you need to see what it's actually doing, not just assume it's doing what you designed.

That's the problem I set out to design for. Not "add a testing feature," but: give people a way to trust their agent, and catch the moment it stops deserving that trust.

The problem, and what surprised me

Insight

Nobody agreed on what "agent" even meant yet — including engineers.

This industry is new enough that I assumed familiarity where there wasn't any. We work on agentic features every day, at a tech company, surrounded by people who talk about evaluators and thresholds like common nouns. It was easy to assume that anyone with a technical background would already know this vocabulary.

Then I talked to actual developers building on our platform and found out they weren't sure what an "eval" was, either. That surprised me more than it should have. It's not just an AECO-industry gap, it's an industry-wide one: a 2025 LangChain survey of AI-agent builders found observability adoption far outpacing evals adoption, with evals still catching up in awareness even among people already shipping agentic systems. And a 2025 RICS construction survey found a lack of skilled personnel to be the most cited barrier to AI adoption industry-wide, a proxy for the same literacy gap.

If the people building the tools didn't have a shared vocabulary for evaluation yet, there was no chance our end users (architects, designers, small business owners) would either.

Insight

This isn't a simple feature. It's a technical workflow wearing a UI.

To run a real evaluation, a user has to choose which evaluators to apply, set a success threshold for each one, and curate a test set to run them against. That's a genuinely technical workflow, no matter how you dress it up.

We had two user types in mind from the start: product teams with an AI engineer or developer who'd curate test sets and run evals with precision, and less technical users who just needed a basic guardrail — some assurance the agent wouldn't embarrass them — without needing to understand the machinery underneath. Designing one feature that could hold both of those users, without making the first group feel constrained or the second group feel lost, was the actual design problem.

A small team, moving fast, with real constraints

I was the lead — and only — designer on this feature. We were a roughly twenty-person team operating at startup pace inside a much larger company, moving fast enough that we didn't have time for the kind of deep user research this problem probably deserved. There was a window, and we didn't want to lose it to process.

There was also a technical ceiling I had to design around, not against: the evaluation backend only supported single-turn conversations at the time, without support for tools running locally. That constraint shaped what v1 could honestly promise, independent of how simple or complex the UI looked.

First move: I built the version everyone else already had

The first pass, in Figma, followed the same pattern as the agent-creation flow it sat next to — a long technical form, no wizard steps, referencing how competitor products in the market handled the same problem. It made sense at the time: it was consistent with the rest of the product, and it looked like what everyone else was shipping.

It was also the wrong call. The pattern itself — a dense, form-based, jargon-first flow — is what makes every competing eval tool hard to approach if you don't already know the vocabulary. I'd copied the industry default without questioning whether the industry default was actually good.

The turning point

Two realizations reshaped the design.

First: most people don't know these concepts, and won't learn them by reading a form. The design needed to work for someone who has never heard the word "agent" before — not as a stretch goal, but as the actual bar. That became the design target for v1: rebuild the flow so it could be understood by someone with zero agentic-AI vocabulary, without stripping out the power the technical users needed.

Second: once we watched how people wanted to build test sets, it was clear they'd prefer to evaluate against real interaction data the moment it existed, rather than hand-curating examples from scratch. We couldn't fully act on that yet — importing real agent interaction data was out of scope for the backend at the time — but it told me where the feature needed to grow next, and it reframed how I thought about AI's role in the flow: not as a gimmick, but as a stand-in for the real data we didn't have access to yet.

Looking back, that second realization echoes something Anthropic's own product team has said publicly about their internal workflow: that the eval suite has effectively replaced the PRD as the artifact that defines what "correct" behavior looks like — because real transcripts of what an agent actually did are a more trustworthy definition of quality than a hypothetical spec someone wrote in advance. We arrived at a version of that same conclusion from the design side, independently, just by watching what our users reached for first.

Rebuilding the flow

V1 restructured evaluation creation into five deliberate steps:

1. Select an agent — the eval is always scoped to a specific agent, so nothing later in the flow requires the user to re-explain context the system already has.

2. Select a test set — the primary path lets users pick from an existing test set in their library, or open a side drawer to import one from a device using a provided template, or build one manually in a table. A secondary, lighter-weight path lets AI generate a test set based on the agent's own scenarios — useful for cold start, or for showing a new user what a good test set even looks like, without forcing them to author one from nothing.

I was deliberate about which path was primary and which was secondary here. Test set content is what actually gets measured — it's where user intent and precision matter most, so I didn't want AI-generation to be the default choice quietly deciding what "correct" meant on someone's behalf. AI assists where the cost of a blank page is highest and the stakes of getting it slightly wrong are lowest — which is really the next step, not this one.

3. Select and configure a metric set — choosing which evaluators apply and setting a success threshold for each, the most technical step in the flow, and the one we worked hardest to keep legible rather than remove.

4. Review and run — a single screen that shows the eval job's name and description alongside a full summary of the selected agent, test set, metrics, and thresholds. This is where AI-assist earns its keep: the job's name and description are auto-generated from the configuration, so a user who's happy with the defaults never has to type anything — but every field stays editable for the user who wants to be precise. It's the moment the whole flow pays off: by the time someone reaches this screen, they've made only the decisions that actually mattered, and they see the entire configuration reflected back before committing.

5. Run.

Shipping it

When I presented the redesigned version to product leadership, it went through without a single revision requested — a first for a feature of this complexity. One stakeholder said they wished this version had existed earlier, because it would have made the concept of agent evaluation far easier to explain to a broader audience inside the company.

That reaction told me the redesign had done its job — not because it added capability, but because it removed the burden of needing to already understand agentic AI just to try it.

A reflection on building this

The hardest part of this feature wasn't any single screen — it was resisting the instinct to design for the audience I was surrounded by every day. I work on agentic AI constantly, in a company full of people fluent in its vocabulary, and it was genuinely easy to assume that baseline in everyone else. The people who corrected that assumption weren't even our least technical users — they were developers.

The backend constraints that shaped v1 (single-turn only, no local tool support, no real interaction-data import) are being addressed now. The next iteration will support building test sets directly from imported agent logs, and evaluating multi-turn conversations rather than single exchanges only. That's the piece I keep coming back to: if real interaction data becomes the primary way people build test sets, evaluation stops being something a designer or PM specifies in advance and starts being something the agent's own usage defines, closer to Anthropic's own observation that the eval suite, not the spec doc, is what actually ends up describing what "correct" means. I didn't design toward that conclusion. I watched users reach for it, and now the backend is catching up to let them.

This is the thread that runs through most of my work: technology tends to arrive more capable than it is legible, and the gap between those two things is where people get left behind. Evaluation is a hard concept even for engineers, which meant the real design problem was never the eval mechanics themselves, it was translation: taking something powerful and technical and making it feel obvious to someone encountering it for the first time. That's the standard I keep applying, on this feature and elsewhere: if it isn't intuitive, it isn't finished.
